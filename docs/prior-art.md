# Prior art: what exists, what it covers, and what it leaves open

This is the long form of the README's prior-art summary. It asks what systems already exist for running fleets of agents, which of pomtree's concerns each covers, and what none of them cover, and it states why that justifies a separate project. Product claims were checked against the vendors' own documentation pages on 2026-11-04 (see Sources); a claim about what a page does not describe is a statement about the page, not about the product. Mechanism-level comparisons against the code are in [`docs/tasks.md`](tasks.md) section 11. This file compares whole systems; section 3.6 summarizes where pomtree deliberately departs from common practice.

## 1. The problem space

Pomtree aims to be a pluggable framework: a small core that handles a short list of hard safety problems out of the box and forces the user to make the remaining safety decisions explicitly, while workflows, agent templates, dashboards and adapters are supplied by the user. That is the intent, not the current state: the code forces some decisions today, several silent defaults remain, and several ports are hardwired. [`docs/safety-decisions.md`](safety-decisions.md) owns the first two facts, listing the forced decisions and the remaining silent defaults, and [`docs/extending.md`](extending.md) the third. The core's list is custody of terminal results until a live owner collects them (scoped in section 4), nested budgets held as reservations at the model proxy, per-task credentials, fencing by generation, and git custody. The design is in [`SPEC.md`](../SPEC.md). The question here is whether those problems are already solved elsewhere.

Unsupervised agent fleets fail in four recurring ways. Spend runs away, because a looping agent or a fan-out of children has no ceiling smaller than the account's. Authority leaks, because children inherit the parent's credentials, network and files and can use them for purposes the parent never intended. Work is orphaned or lost: a parent crashes or exits while children run, or a child finishes and nobody is left to receive its result, and the output is deleted by a retention timer or never produced. Finally, state is lost or corrupted, because a replacement for a presumed-dead agent runs while the original is still writing, or because an agent's working tree is changed with no checkpoint to return to. Each failure is mundane and recurs across agent products.

These failures cut across layers. A durable workflow engine addresses lost state but not spend. A hosted agent platform addresses spend but not authority between sibling agents. The comparison below is therefore organized by dimension rather than by product, to show which layer each system occupies.

## 2. Comparison by dimension

Seven dimensions matter for the problems above: who owns whose lifetime, whether a result survives until someone takes it, whether money is capped and at what granularity, whose credentials an agent holds, what isolates it, who is custodian of its working files, and how a user extends the system. Table 1 gives the answer for one representative of each family; the text after it carries the evidence and the qualifications that do not fit in cells. A cell reading "not documented" means the pages listed in Sources were read and do not describe the feature; it is not a claim that the product lacks it.

*Table 1. One representative per family. The agent-in-container column is a general characterization (secondary report, not checked against the primary source). The last column states the intended design.*

**Lifecycle and ownership.** The mature models come from infrastructure, not from agent products. A Temporal child workflow carries a Parent Close Policy chosen per child: terminate (the default), request-cancel, or abandon [2]; children do not carry over when a parent continues as new [1]. Restate cancels non-blockingly, leaving compensation to the handlers, and kills an entire invocation tree without compensation as a last resort [12]. Ray ties an actor's life to its creator: an actor fate-shares with its owner, and Ray will not recover an actor whose owner is dead even with restarts configured, while a detached actor has no owner and lives until the cluster is destroyed [23]. These show that the question "what happens to children when their parent ends" has several established answers. None of them is "the child's result remains an obligation of the parent until the parent accounts for it", which is the POSIX `wait` shape that `docs/tasks.md` section 11.1 identifies as the closest precedent.

**Durability of results.** Durable engines persist what happens, but they delete it on a schedule, not on acknowledgment. Temporal keeps closed-workflow data for a namespace retention period, after which a timer task cleans it up, and the page read describes nothing that holds a result until an owner acknowledges it [7]. Restate keeps completed invocations for a configurable period and lets the user purge them [12]. In the agent frameworks, a LangGraph checkpointer persists graph state per thread for conversation continuity, human-in-the-loop workflows and fault tolerance, and the page recommends a persistent checkpointer such as PostgresSaver for production [14]; OpenAI Agents SDK sessions persist conversation history across runs in a choice of backends [16]. These preserve an agent's own state, not a child's terminal result for a parent. Managed Agents stores a session's history and sandbox state server-side and lets the user delete them [17], while its webhooks are stated not to be a durable log: after three failed delivery attempts an event is dropped with no signal [21]. Ray's objects show the failure this dimension guards against: object metadata lives with the owner, Ray does not support recovery from owner failure, and later reads raise `OwnerDiedError` [24].

**Budgets.** Dollar caps are the dimension where the nearest competitor is closest. Managed Agents lets the user set a hard cap per session, priced at public list rates and checked before each model request; the request in flight when the cap is crossed completes, so overshoot is bounded by one request per thread. A multiagent session has a single budget shared across all threads, with no per-thread caps [19]. A budgeted session is rejected at creation if it uses a model with no public list price, which limits it to priced models [19]. No durable engine or framework page read documents a spend cap, and none of the Temporal pages read documents a nested dollar cap. The precedent for nested allocation is therefore in resource schedulers, not in agent systems; see section 3.5.

**Credentials.** In Managed Agents, "all agents share the same sandbox, filesystem, and vault credentials", and vault credentials are session-scoped [18]. The page offers a partial narrowing: an agent definition declares only the MCP servers it needs, and an inline agent, which has no definition, uses those of the agent that runs the session [18]. That limits tool reach, not the credentials the sandbox holds. None of the Temporal pages read documents per-task provider credentials. The LangGraph and OpenAI sessions pages do not describe per-task credentials; the OpenAI page notes that a session ID does not authenticate a user or authorize access to the history, so the application must [14][16]. Macaroons are the standard precedent for attenuated delegation, where each hand-off can only narrow authority [28]; no agent system surveyed here uses the idea.

**Sandbox and workspace custody.** Sandboxing in this field comes from the product, not the orchestrator. The Temporal pages read describe no sandbox of their own, and its OpenAI Agents integration for the Python SDK runs model calls as activities, with sandbox support stated to be pre-release and delegated to remote clients such as Daytona or E2B [8]. Managed Agents offers an Anthropic-managed cloud sandbox or a self-hosted one, shared by the threads of a session [17][18]. Container-per-agent tools run each agent in its own container and worktree managers give each agent a git worktree with no sandbox; in general each covers one half, and neither is tree-structured. That characterization is a secondary report, not checked against the primary source. None of the pages read describes git custody, meaning checkpoints that hold ids and hashes and a defined merge path back to a parent.

**Extension model.** The engines extend through code in an SDK. Temporal documents an OpenAI Agents integration for the Python SDK [8]; Restate documents integrations with the Vercel AI SDK, OpenAI Agents SDK, Google ADK, Pydantic AI and LangChain, or a custom loop with model calls wrapped in `ctx.run()` for any LLM SDK [13]. Managed Agents extends through agent definitions, MCP servers and skills [17]. In both cases the user gets a rich extension surface and no enforced safety contract: nothing requires a workflow to declare a budget, a credential scope or a result owner. Pomtree's stance is the inverse: the extension surface is deliberately narrow ports and adapters, and the intent is that the core refuses to run work that has not made the safety decisions explicit. Today it forces the decisions listed in [`docs/safety-decisions.md`](safety-decisions.md) and several ports are hardwired (see [`docs/extending.md`](extending.md)).

## 3. Notes by family

### 3.1 Durable-execution engines

Temporal and Restate cover the lifecycle and cancellation layer well. Temporal's server is MIT-licensed [11], ships built-in Schedules [3], and offers a Parent Close Policy per child [2]. Its limits are those of a general engine: the child-workflows page says a single parent should not spawn more than 1,000 children, the event-history page states that an execution is terminated past 51,200 events or 10,000 signals, and activities receive cancellation only when they heartbeat [1][4][5][6]. Those figures are as published on 2026-11-04 and are configuration-dependent; treat them as illustrative. A production deployment needs persistent data stores, monitoring and, for availability, multi-cluster replication, and the `start-dev` server is not for production [9][10]. Restate documents durable LLM calls and tool executions and human approvals that pause an agent and resume across restarts [13]. Inngest's durable steps persist completed step results and retry failing steps on their own; its docs state that a step does not make an external action happen exactly once and recommend idempotency keys for writes, payments and messages, and the page read does not mention agents [15]. In `docs/tasks.md` section 11.1, Temporal and Restate are mechanism precedent for close policies, heartbeat progress details and the cancel/kill/purge ladder.

### 3.2 Agent frameworks

LangGraph, the OpenAI Agents SDK and their peers are libraries that run an agent loop inside a host process and persist its state. LangGraph's persistence page describes checkpointers, thread-scoped state, stores for cross-thread memory, and subgraphs that each manage their own checkpoint namespace; it does not mention budgets, sandboxing, credentials, or cancelling a subgraph [14]. The OpenAI Agents SDK page describes sessions with many backends, including SQLite, Redis, SQLAlchemy and Dapr, and states that the session ID does not authenticate access; budgets and sandboxing are not described on it [16]. These frameworks are good places for a user's workflow logic to live, and either can run as an agent inside a pomtree task; they do not address a fleet of such agents.

### 3.3 Vendor-hosted agent platforms

Claude Managed Agents is the closest comparable, because it covers spend, a sandbox and a durable session history in one hosted product (beta at the time of reading, behind the `managed-agents-2026-04-01` header) [17]. What the pages read establish: subagents run as threads inside one session, with isolated conversation context but a shared sandbox, filesystem and vault credentials [18]; one session budget is shared by all threads, with no per-thread cap [19]; outcomes add a grader with a rubric and a bounded number of revision iterations, which evaluates quality, not spend or custody [20]; webhooks are not a durable log [21]; and the session event history is persisted server-side and can be listed in full [17][22]. An environment can be a self-hosted sandbox on the user's own infrastructure [17]. What the pages read do not describe: per-subagent budgets, per-subagent credentials, custody of a child's result for its parent, or git custody. Absence from these pages is the evidence; the product is in beta and changes, so the triggers in section 5 name it first. Consumer agent products are for a different user and are not compared here.

### 3.4 Agent-in-container and worktree tools

These are the open tools that put each agent in a container or git worktree: terminal agent teams, worktree managers, container runners, open agent platforms and sandbox providers. As a general characterization, isolation is per agent and aggregating spend or results is the user's job. This family is a secondary report, not checked against the primary source, so no claim about a specific tool is made here, and its column in Table 1 is labeled accordingly. Sandbox providers such as E2B and Daytona are infrastructure that a pomtree `Sandbox` port could in principle wrap, not competitors.

### 3.5 OS and cluster schedulers as mechanism precedent

The oldest systems solved the hardest parts of the lifecycle problem, and they are the source of most of pomtree's mechanisms. POSIX `wait` consumes a terminated child's status and returns immediately if it is already available [30]; when a process exits, its children are reparented to an implementation-defined system process [31]. Nomad separates stopping a task from destroying it: waiting on a task is valid after it is stopped and returns the exit result, and only destruction removes the task's state [25]. HTCondor lets the application write its own checkpoint and exit with a declared exit code, after which it restarts the job [26]. Kubernetes finalizers block deletion until cleanup completes [27]. The Debezium outbox pattern gives at-least-once publication with consumer-side duplicate detection [29]. Macaroons contribute attenuation [28]. What these systems have in common is that they are mechanisms for processes and containers; none carries a dollar budget, an LLM credential or a git history, and none were designed for a workload whose resource consumption is priced per request and whose principals are models. The line-by-line comparison against the code, including where pomtree deliberately differs, is in `docs/tasks.md` section 11.2. That a design reuses established mechanisms is a reason to expect it to be sound; it does not by itself establish that it is.

### 3.6 Where pomtree diverges from established practice

A few departures from common practice are deliberate. A reservation left unsettled when the proxy or daemon crashes is charged in full, where a payment authorization would simply release the hold: the provider may already have served the call, so releasing would let spend vanish from the books. Provider overage beyond a reservation is written off up the tree to a possibly negative root, where a hard cap such as a cgroup limit would refuse the allocation: here the money is already spent, so the honest record is a deficit that blocks further reservations. A parent may not finish successfully while a child is unreaped, where POSIX lets a parent exit with unwaited children that are reparented: a child's result is an obligation until someone accounts for it. Budget exhaustion escalates a spending limit up a tree of principals, for which no existing system was found. Credentials use an in-house signed token and a single per-agent revocation cutoff in place of JWT with token ids, because yield and resume revoke and re-issue constantly and only the daemon's own components accept the tokens; and the provider key is read only from a file path, never an environment variable, because sandboxed agents and process listings must never see it. Each departure trades a convention's familiarity for a property the rest of the design depends on.

## 4. What none of them cover

The claim, with its scope: among the systems surveyed here and read from primary pages on 2026-11-04 (Temporal, Restate, Inngest, Ray, LangGraph persistence, OpenAI Agents SDK sessions, Claude Managed Agents), none combines tree custody, nested dollar caps and sandboxed git checkpoints. Specifically, no surveyed system documents all of the following together: a parent that holds each child's terminal result until it collects it; a dollar budget carved from the parent's and enforced before each model request, per branch; credentials issued per task, not per session; and a working tree whose checkpoints can be restored and fenced against a superseded generation. The scope is the pages read; it excludes the agent-in-container tools (secondary report, not checked against the primary source) and any private or unreleased feature.

Temporal and Restate cover the lifecycle and cancellation layer and are viable orchestrator backends, not substitutes for the rest. A pomtree deployment could keep its task tree in either, but that would still leave the proxy-enforced reservations, per-task keys, sandbox and git custody to build, and an engine's own fencing would not by itself extend to the model key or to storage. Managed Agents covers spend and sandboxing for one vendor's session model, and its documented gaps (a single shared budget, shared credentials and sandbox, history deletable at will) are the ones pomtree targets. Whether to call that combination necessary is a judgment: users who run a single agent, or who accept one shared cap per session, need none of it, and for them Managed Agents or a framework is the simpler choice.

This is what justifies a separate project. The argument, an inference and not a measurement, is that custody, budgets and fencing must hold across crashes, restarts and replacements, so they are hard to add after the fact to a system whose core does not own them. A framework whose core owns them is a different thing from a library in which they are optional features; the aim that it also refuses to run work that skips the remaining decisions is only partly met (the forced decisions are listed in [`docs/safety-decisions.md`](safety-decisions.md)). The case is that of Pi: a small core with a strict contract that other things plug into, applied to orchestration. The design is ahead of the build in places. One gap is specific to the custody claim above. A child's result is held unreaped for its owner except for children of the root supervisor: `finishTail` in `packages/pomtreed/src/daemon/lifecycle.ts` reaps an exited child of the root at once, and `findZombies` in `packages/pomtreed/src/lifecycle.ts` excludes root children. Reap only moves the agent to `reaped`: the store keeps the record and its result, and what reap ends is the hold, meaning the budget claim, the zombie alert and the parent's obligation to collect. Children of the root are therefore persisted and delivered (`pomtree show` and `pomtree wait` read the record; the CLI calls are in `packages/pomtree/src/cli.ts`). For a dead parent the code does not reap: its exited, unreaped descendants are held for the nearest live ancestor (the custodian, the operator when the chain reaches the root), who is told once and can read and reap them through the API (`holdForCustodian` and `cancelChildren` in `packages/pomtreed/src/daemon/lifecycle.ts`). A parent may instead declare `reap-on-exit` or a hold TTL, in which case the reap follows the notice. The differentiator is custody until a live owner acknowledges, with delivery and escalation, not retention of bytes.

## 5. Reopen triggers

This comparison should be revisited if any of the following occurs. Managed Agents gains per-subagent or per-thread budgets, per-thread credentials, or custody of a thread's result for its parent: the budgets page currently states no per-thread caps, and the multiagent page states shared credentials. Managed Agents budgets are extended beyond models with a public list price. Temporal or Restate ships native sandboxing, per-task credentials, nested dollar budgets or a result-acknowledgment retention mode. A framework such as LangGraph or the OpenAI Agents SDK documents budgets with nested scopes. An agent-in-container tool documents nested caps and result custody. A decision to adopt Temporal or Restate as an orchestrator backend would also reopen section 3.1, because it changes which layer is built. Each trigger should be checked against the pages cited, with a new access date.

## 6. Limits of this survey

Numeric limits are omitted or labeled illustrative because they are configuration and may change. The Managed Agents multiagent and events pages are long and were checked by targeted extraction of the sandbox, credential, budget and history statements, not end to end. The Restate AI page was read at index level, not each sub-page. Ray was read on the actor and object fault-tolerance pages only. Agent-in-container tools and consumer products are a secondary report, not checked against the primary source. The Gneiting and Raftery paper cited by `docs/economy.md` was confirmed from bibliographic records, not its full text.
